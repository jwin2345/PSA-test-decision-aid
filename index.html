PSA Screening Decision Tool

PSA Screening Decision Tool

Research version 0.22.2

Estimates life expectancy and risk of metastatic prostate cancer for a man aged 65–84 who is already being screened, and indicates whether stopping PSA screening is reasonable.

  • Inclusion criteria: men 65–84, PSA below 4 ng/mL, at least one prior PSA, no prostate cancer diagnosis
  • Exclusion criteria: symptoms, abnormal DRE, PSA 4 ng/mL or higher
  • Built from: TriNetX health-system records and the US life table
Research use only. This tool has not yet been validated in individual patient data and should not be the sole basis for a screening decision. Use it to support shared decision-making.

Intended use

The tool is for men aged 65 to 84 who are already in a PSA screening pattern: at least one earlier PSA, a current PSA below 4 ng/mL, and no diagnosis of prostate cancer. It is not for men with urinary or bone symptoms, an abnormal digital rectal examination, or a PSA of 4 ng/mL or higher, who need diagnostic evaluation rather than a screening decision. Its output is meant to inform a shared decision; it does not replace clinical judgment or patient preference.

Decision rule

Guidelines tie stopping screening to age (USPSTF: 70) or to life expectancy. The tool estimates the two quantities that age stands in for and applies two tests.

  • Life expectancy. Consider stopping if estimated life expectancy is below 15 years. The European Association of Urology gives a strong recommendation that men with less than 15 years are unlikely to benefit from early diagnosis1. The threshold can be lowered to 10 years, the American Cancer Society and NCCN position2. The current AUA/SUO guideline lists life expectancy as one factor in shared decision-making without a numeric threshold3.
  • Metastatic cancer risk. Consider stopping if his lifetime risk of metastatic prostate cancer beyond a 4-year lead time is no higher than that of a low-risk reference man: a 60-year-old with PSA below 1 ng/mL, the group the Malmö cohort identified as able to stop or test rarely4. The reference man is given the patient's own comorbidity, so the comparison reflects cancer risk at equal health; life expectancy is judged by the first test alone.

Both comparisons sometimes land close to their thresholds, and the tool says so rather than printing a bare verdict. A result is marked borderline when the criterion that decided it sits inside the model's own uncertainty: for the cancer comparison, a gap to the reference man smaller than the model's own 95% uncertainty for that comparison (about a factor of 1.37 either way, from 1,000 simulations that vary every measured input); for life expectancy, within 1.5 years of the threshold. The recommendation itself does not change; the label is there because a difference that small is not a finding.

Otherwise the result is to continue screening. A PSA of 4 ng/mL or higher is outside the tool's range.

Data source

All inputs come from the TriNetX research network: electronic health records from about 125 health-care organizations, mostly US academic systems, accessed as aggregate counts, survival curves and model coefficients only (no patient-level data). Each cohort was defined as men with a PSA in the target age and value range, an earlier PSA at least 6 months before, no prior prostate cancer diagnosis or recorded treatment for it, and a health-system visit at least 1 year before the index PSA. Analyses were run in October 2026.

Life expectancy

Average survival comes from the 2023 Social Security period life table for US men5. Each patient's death hazard is expressed relative to that average and applied by the proportional-hazards identity:

S(t) = [ l(age + t) / l(age) ] HR    life expectancy = ∫ S(t) dt

The hazard ratio has two parts. The baseline, for a man with none of the listed conditions, is the 10-year death rate of TriNetX men with none of them recorded divided (on the log scale) by that of all tested men in the same age band, about 0.5. Each condition then multiplies the hazard by its ratio from Cox models for death fitted separately in men aged 65–74 and 75–84 (21 conditions, mutually adjusted, PSA 1–3.99 ng/mL; 694,193 and 226,023 index tests), interpolated by age. The baseline is corrected for low albumin and BMI below 25, which could not be excluded from the condition-free cohorts. This approach follows published comorbidity-adjusted life tables6.

Men who keep having PSA tests are healthier than the average man of their age12, so the average tested man is not given the life-table death rate. Among men whose routine laboratory results (LDL cholesterol) were recorded in the network, 10-year death was 13.9% in PSA-tested men and 27.1% in never-tested men at age 67 (hazard ratio 0.43), and 35.3% and 50.5% at age 77 (hazard ratio 0.53). Scaled to the life table, PSA-tested men die at about 0.60 times the population rate at 67 and 0.66 times at 77 (0.56–0.63 and 0.62–0.70 depending on how many deaths the records miss). This factor is applied to every man, including the reference man, at his current age, linearly between 67 and 77 and flat outside; the condition hazard ratios then spread individual men around the average tested man. It assumes the advantage of tested over untested men is the same for every comorbidity.

Metastatic cancer risk

The calculator shows metastatic risk mainly as a ratio to the low-risk reference man. Absolute lifetime risks are shown as approximate values, because they rest on coded diagnoses and the corrections described below, whereas the same definition applies to the patient and the reference man and largely cancels from the ratio.

The cancer outcome is a first recorded diagnosis of metastatic prostate cancer: a prostate cancer code (ICD-10 C61) together with a code for secondary malignancy of lymph nodes, respiratory or digestive organs, bone or other sites, or a secondary neuroendocrine tumor (C77, C78, C79 or C7B), both after the index PSA. Metastasis codes alone are not used, because in these cohorts nearly 90% of new metastasis codes were in men without a prostate cancer code.

The yearly rates come from 12 competing-risks cohorts (index PSA at age 67, 72, 77 or 81–82 × PSA below 1, 1–1.99 and 2–3.99 ng/mL), with follow-up to about 19 years at 67 and 9 years at 81–82, refined within bands by six narrow PSA bands (0.5–0.99 up to 3–3.99 ng/mL) indexed at age 72 in the same way. In these cohorts, and for the reference man (index PSA at age 60), men are excluded if prostate cancer, a personal history of it, prostatectomy, prostate radiation or brachytherapy, or androgen-deprivation drugs were coded at or before the index age. The competing-risks analyses give the year-by-year timing of a first androgen-receptor pathway inhibitor (ARPI) prescription, a marker of advanced disease. Each cohort's yearly ARPI rate is converted to a metastatic rate by that cohort's own ratio of men with coded metastatic prostate cancer to men starting an ARPI (1.5 to 2.7 across the twelve cohorts; 2.8 for the reference man; 3,837 against 1,642 overall). Both counts come from the same men over the same follow-up, so loss to follow-up affects them equally and cancels from the ratio. Every rate is then multiplied by 0.44, the share of coded metastatic prostate cancer that remains when men with any other primary cancer are excluded (measured against the same exclusion in the cohort denominator: 0.50 for the reference man and 0.44, 0.36 and 0.43 at index ages 67, 72 and 77, with no trend by age). Because the same factor applies to the patient and the reference man, it sets the displayed level and does not change the recommendation. The model assumes that metastatic diagnoses follow the same timing as ARPI starts within each cohort; a direct person-time estimate of the metastatic rate is in progress.

These rates are applied year by year against the patient's own survival from the life-expectancy estimate7, so a man likely to die sooner accumulates less risk. Metastases within the next 4 years are not counted, because a cancer that will surface that soon is unlikely to be changed by a test today; the 4 years reflect the delay before the screening benefit in ERSPC8–10. Beyond each cohort's follow-up, the rate is taken from the next older cohort at the same attained age (a 67-year-old's year 20 uses the 72-year-old cohort's year 15); the oldest cohort, with none older, carries forward the average of its last 3 measured years.

Comorbidities act only through survival; in a Cox model for ARPI start in men 65–84 they did not change the rate. The within-band PSA correction was measured for ARPI start and is assumed to apply to metastatic disease. Family history, race, prior biopsy and other prostate cancer risk factors are not inputs: their hazard ratios were available only for ARPI start.

The estimated chance of dying of prostate cancer weights each metastatic diagnosis by the share of men with a first metastatic code who die of the disease: 1 minus their 10-year survival relative to the life table, 35% when the first metastasis is at 65–74 (45,861 men) and 29% at 75–84 (31,167 men), excluding men with 11 common other cancers. It is shown for context and does not enter the recommendation.

Checks and external comparison

  • Death-model calibration. In 13 cohorts of men with specified conditions at ages 70–79, chosen before outcomes were seen, the calibration slope for predicted against observed 10-year death was 0.87 (95% CI, 0.79–0.96), fitted through the origin, and observed/expected deaths were 0.95 (95% CI, 0.93–0.98). These cohorts also informed model development, so this is not an independent validation.
  • Metastatic disease in men with comorbidities. In the same cohorts, coded metastatic prostate cancer was 0.72 times (95% CI, 0.67–0.78) the rate expected relative to men with no conditions, so the tool may overstate cancer risk in men with comorbidities by about a quarter (fewer diagnoses may also reflect less investigation). Applying this factor changes 443 of 4,680 example recommendations, all toward stopping.
  • PSA effect at older ages. The within-band PSA correction measured at age 72 was tested in advance at age 77 in three narrow bands (10-year ARPI start), with the rule that all three 95% CIs must contain the prediction. Two did (PSA 0.5–0.99: predicted 0.33%, observed 0.43%; PSA 3–3.99: 1.35% vs 1.38%) and one did not (PSA 1.5–1.99: predicted 0.71%, observed 0.44%, 95% CI 0.27–0.69%, 28 events). The uncorrected prediction also missed, so the failure is not caused by the correction, and at 5 years the three bands are in the expected order.
  • Outcome definition. Using first ARPI prescription as the outcome instead of coded metastatic disease changes 245 of 4,680 example recommendations (ages 65–84 × 9 PSA values × 26 health patterns), all toward continuing.
  • Comparison with an age-70 cutoff. Across the same 4,680 examples the tool and a rule of screening to 70 disagree in 22.7%: 40.0% of examples under 70 would be told they can stop, and 17.0% of examples aged 70 or older would be told to continue.
  • Other cancers in the metastatic definition. In the reference man's cohort and at index ages 67, 72 and 77 (PSA below 1), men with no other primary cancer code had 36% to 50% of the coded metastatic rate (pooled 44%), so more than half of coded metastatic prostate cancer occurs in men who also have another cancer. The pooled factor is applied (see Metastatic cancer risk); applying age-specific factors instead moves the first stopping age by up to 2.6 years, within their confidence intervals.
  • Comparison with CISNET. For PSA-tested men, estimated life expectancy falls below 15 years at age 80.5 with no comorbidities, 77.5 with diabetes, 75.0 with COPD and 71.2 with heart failure and COPD, 4 to 9 years later than the comorbidity-specific screening cessation ages from CISNET models (76, 72, 66 and 66)11, which use population survival. Without the tested-men correction the ages are 75.7, 73.3, 70.6 and 66.7. The criteria differ, so this is a comparison rather than a validation.

Limitations

  • Built from aggregate data. Individual-level calibration, discrimination and decision-curve analysis are still needed.
  • The tested-men survival factor was measured at two ages, in men with recorded laboratory results, and depends on how completely deaths are recorded; it is assumed to be the same for every comorbidity. Deaths missing from health-record data could also bias the condition hazard ratios in either direction13.
  • Hazard ratios were measured over 10 years of follow-up and are applied for life. Combined hazard ratios for many conditions are multiplied without interaction terms.
  • Metastatic disease is identified from diagnosis codes, which are incomplete and can be dated late. The correction for other cancers (0.44) also removes some true prostate metastases in men with a second cancer, so it may understate risk. After correction the reference man reaches 1.5% by age 85, against 0.5% reported for similar men in Malmö4; lymph-node codes at surgery and differences in era and imaging may account for the rest. Because the same definition applies to the patient and the reference man, the comparison is less affected than the displayed level. The metastatic rate is derived from ARPI timing scaled by each cohort's count ratio, not yet measured directly over person-time.
  • Men with comorbidities had fewer recorded metastatic diagnoses than predicted (see Checks); the tool does not adjust for this.
  • Part of the lifetime risk, including about half of the reference man's, lies beyond measured follow-up and is extrapolated. PSA values below 0.6 or above about 3.5 ng/mL are treated as the nearest measured band.
  • MRI, PSA density, grade history and DRE findings are not inputs.
